# Your rights at a protest (research draft, checked 9 October 2026)

General legal information, not legal advice. Every point below rests on the source in `rights.json`, which carries the verbatim quote and the address opened.

- **1. Right to protest.** Every citizen has a right to speak out and to gather with others peacefully and without weapons. (Constitution of India, Article 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(b))
- **1. Limits on that right.** The government can put reasonable limits on gatherings by law, for public order or the sovereignty and integrity of India, but only reasonable ones. (Constitution of India, Article 19(3) (assembly); Article 19(2) (speech))
- **1. Peaceful protest is a basic feature of democracy.** The Supreme Court has said that peaceful protest is part of democracy and the government should respect it. (In re Ramlila Maidan Incident v. Home Secretary, Supreme Court, 23 February 2012, para 200 (Swatanter Kumar J.))
- **2. Prohibitory orders: who and how long.** A District Magistrate, Sub-divisional Magistrate or a specially empowered Executive Magistrate can issue a written order banning certain acts in an area. It lasts at most two months, unless the State Government extends it by up to six months more. (Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, section 163(1), (3), (4) (replaces CrPC section 144))
- **2. Prohibitory orders: you can ask for one to be cancelled.** Anyone affected can ask the Magistrate (or the State Government, for an extension) to cancel or change the order, and must be given a chance to be heard. (BNSS 2023, section 163(5) and (7))
- **2. Disobeying a prohibitory order.** Knowingly disobeying a lawful prohibitory order is itself an offence. (Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, section 223 (replaces IPC section 188))
- **2. Supreme Court tests for prohibitory orders.** The Supreme Court has said these orders are for emergencies, cannot be used to suppress legitimate protest, must state the facts behind them, must use the least restrictive measure, and cannot be repeated again and again. (Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India, Supreme Court, 10 January 2020, para 140 (AIR 2020 SC 1308; (2020) 3 SCC 637))
- **2. Emergencies only.** The Court has also said such orders apply only in emergencies, though authorities may control the time and place of a meeting. (In re Ramlila Maidan Incident, Supreme Court, 23 February 2012)
- **3. What an unlawful assembly is.** A gathering of five or more people becomes an 'unlawful assembly' only if their shared aim is something like using criminal force against the government or officials, resisting the law, committing an offence, or forcing people by criminal force. A gathering that starts lawful can become unlawful later. (Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, section 189(1) and Explanation (replaces IPC section 141))
- **3. Staying after an order to disperse.** Knowingly joining or staying in an unlawful assembly is punishable; staying after it has been lawfully ordered to disperse carries a higher punishment. Staying in any gathering of five or more likely to disturb the peace after a lawful order to disperse is also an offence. (BNS 2023, section 189(2), (3) and (5))
- **3. Who can order a crowd to disperse.** An Executive Magistrate, the officer in charge of the police station or, in his absence, a police officer of at least sub-inspector rank can order an unlawful assembly, or a gathering of five or more likely to disturb the peace, to disperse. Members then have a duty to leave; if they do not, force may be used. (BNSS 2023, section 148(1)-(2) (replaces CrPC section 129))
- **4. Protest areas cannot simply be banned.** The Supreme Court held that a blanket ban on protests at Jantar Mantar and the Boat Club in Delhi was not allowed; the police may regulate protests there with guidelines and permission, not ban them altogether. (Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan v. Union of India, Supreme Court, 23 July 2018, paras 69-70 (AIR 2018 SC 3476))
- **4. Public roads cannot be occupied indefinitely.** The Supreme Court held that protesters cannot block a public road or occupy a public space indefinitely, and that protests must be held at designated places. (Amit Sahni v. Commissioner of Police, Supreme Court, 7 October 2020 (AIR 2020 SC 4704))
- **4. Permission rules vary by place.** Whether you need police permission, and where you may protest, is set by state police Acts and local orders, and differs from state to state and city to city. Check with the local police station or a lawyer before the day. (Example: the Delhi prohibitory order considered in Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan (2018))
- **5. Told why you are arrested.** If arrested, you must be told the grounds of arrest as soon as possible, and you can consult and be defended by a lawyer of your choice. (Constitution of India, Article 22(1))
- **5. Before a magistrate within 24 hours.** You must be taken before a magistrate within 24 hours of arrest (not counting travel time), and cannot be held longer without the magistrate's order. (Constitution of India, Article 22(2); BNSS 2023, sections 57 and 58)
- **5. Full particulars of the offence, and bail.** Police arresting without a warrant must immediately tell you the full details of the offence. For a bailable offence they must tell you that you can be released on bail. (BNSS 2023, section 47 (replaces CrPC section 50))
- **5. Someone you name must be told.** The police must at once tell a relative, friend or other person you name that you have been arrested and where you are being held, and tell you of this right at the police station. (BNSS 2023, section 48(1)-(2) (replaces CrPC section 50A))
- **5. Officer identification and arrest memo.** The arresting officer must wear a clear name identification and prepare an arrest memo, witnessed by a family member or a respectable local person and signed by you. (BNSS 2023, section 36 (replaces CrPC section 41B); D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal, (1997) 1 SCC 416, decided 18 December 1996, requirements (1)-(2))
- **5. Meeting a lawyer during questioning.** You may meet a lawyer of your choice during police questioning, though not for the whole of it. (BNSS 2023, section 38 (replaces CrPC section 41D); D.K. Basu requirement (10))
- **5. Notice to appear instead of arrest.** For offences punishable with up to seven years, police must issue a notice to appear instead of arresting you when arrest is not necessary. If you comply with the notice, you should not be arrested unless the officer records reasons. (BNSS 2023, section 35(3)-(6) (replaces CrPC section 41A))
- **5. Medical examination.** After arrest you must be examined by a government medical officer, or a registered medical practitioner if none is available, soon after the arrest. (BNSS 2023, section 53(1) (replaces CrPC section 54))
- **6. Women: no arrest at night, save exceptionally.** A woman should not be arrested after sunset and before sunrise. In exceptional cases a woman police officer must first get written permission from a first-class Magistrate. (BNSS 2023, section 43(5) (replaces CrPC section 46(4)))
- **6. Women: not to be touched by a male officer.** When a woman is arrested, her submission is presumed from being told orally, and a male officer should not touch her unless circumstances require it. Only a woman may search a woman. (BNSS 2023, section 43(1) proviso; section 49(2))
- **7. Free legal aid: the Constitution.** The Constitution directs the State to provide free legal aid so that nobody is denied justice because they are poor or disadvantaged. (Constitution of India, Article 39A)
- **7. Free legal aid: who qualifies.** Anyone in custody is entitled to free legal services, as are all women and children, members of Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, persons with disabilities, industrial workers and people on low incomes. (Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987, section 12 (with section 13))
- **7. NALSA helpline.** The national legal aid helpline number is 15100 (toll-free). (National Legal Services Authority website)

## Uncertain or unverified

- BNSS and BNS text is the Gazette of India (Extraordinary) as enacted on 25 December 2023, hosted by the Ministry of Home Affairs. indiacode.nic.in timed out, so any amendment after enactment was not checked against an updated text.
- BNSS s.1(2): apart from Chapters IX, XI and XII, the Sanhita does not apply to Nagaland and certain tribal areas unless extended. The arrest provisions (Chapter V) may not apply there. Chapter XI (ss.148-163) does apply.
- Supreme Court judgments were read on indiankanoon.org (a judgments site), not on sci.gov.in. SCC citations for Ramlila (2012) 5 SCC 1, MKSS (2018) 17 SCC 324, Amit Sahni (2020) 10 SCC 439 and Anuradha Bhasin (2020) 3 SCC 637 were not displayed on the pages opened; the AIR citations were. D.K. Basu's 1997 (1) SCC 416 was displayed.
- Anuradha Bhasin and Ramlila interpret CrPC s.144. BNSS s.163 reproduces its wording, but no judgment applying them to s.163 was checked.
- Permission requirements under state police Acts and city police orders were not verified for any state; the page should say only that they vary and are set locally.
