In short

  1. Before the revision, the rolls already held 98.3% of adults in the 19 states with population projections. Only 6 of those states had more names on the roll than adults.
  2. The revision still left 13.39 crore names off the draft rolls, the first published versions of the revised rolls, in 31 states. That is 14.1 of every 100 electors, and the draft rolls now hold 84.4% of adults. States whose rolls were most over-full did not cut more.
  3. Where the names removed can be counted seat by seat, in Bihar and Tamil Nadu, they were more than the winning margin, the votes by which the winner beat the runner-up, in 385 of 477 assembly seats. That makes them large enough to have decided those seats, though the lists cannot show that they did. In West Bengal and Kerala only the fall in the roll can be counted, and it was more than the margin in 177 of 434.
  4. Of the names removed with a reason given, only 20.9% were entered as dead. The rest are names an officer marked as moved, missing or on the roll twice.
  5. The same rules applied in every state. Delhi removed 32.8 of every 100 electors and Lakshadweep 2.5. Among the big states, the share marked moved or not found runs from 49.7% in Andhra Pradesh to 91.1% in Delhi.
  6. Women were more likely to be removed. In 8 of the 11 states that report by sex, women are a bigger share of the names removed than of the roll.
  7. Little has come back. In the 21 states with a final roll, the roll published after claims and objections are heard, the rolls grew back by at most 14.1% of what was cut, and that rise includes new voters.

Two limits. The adult figures count people where they live, so states that draw migrants look short of electors who are registered in their home states. And these figures show what happened. They cannot show why it happened, or that anyone meant it to.

The Special Intensive Revision is a fresh check of every name on the electoral rolls, and it has been carried out in three phases. Bihar came first, in 2025, then twelve states and union territories ordered in October 2025, with house visits from November, and eighteen more in 2026. In the 31 that have published a draft roll, 13,39,03,307 electors on the rolls before the revision were not carried onto it. That is 14.1 for every 100. Tripura, the last, is reported to publish its draft in December.

Every state but Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu gave a reason for most of its removals. Of the 13.38 crore names split by reason, 2.80 crore (20.9%) were entered as dead, 9.57 crore (71.5%) as shifted, absent or other, and 1.01 crore (7.6%) as enrolled more than once. Deaths are about one in five. The other four in five rest on an officer's entry that the elector was not where the roll placed them, or was on it twice.

A reason is what an officer wrote down after a visit, or after failing to find the elector, and nobody has checked it. The figures cannot say how many entries were wrong.

State by state, on one map

The rolls against the adult population

A roll larger than the adult population is what a roll swollen by the dead and the departed would look like, and it is the case the revision was meant to answer. Across the 19 states with projections by age, the rolls before the revision held 98.3% of the projected population aged 18 and over, and the draft rolls hold 84.4%. Only 6 had a roll larger than their adult population (Telangana, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Odisha, Andhra Pradesh and Kerala). How far a roll exceeded its adult population has no relationship with how many names the state removed (rank correlation 0.05). A rank correlation runs from -1 to 1 and shows whether states that rank high on one measure also rank high on the other. The lowest draft rolls against the adult population are in Delhi (57.5%), Haryana (76.6%) and Uttar Pradesh (77.7%). The projections count residents, so a state that receives many migrants counts adults who may be registered in their home state, which lowers its ratio.

Set against the 2024 Lok Sabha election, the names left off the draft roll equal a fifth or more of the votes polled in 13 states, led by Delhi (53.2%), Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu (42.6%) and Maharashtra (36.1%).

The three reasons, by phase

The share entered as dead fell from 34.0% in Bihar to 22.3% in phase 2 and 18.1% in phase 3, while the share entered as shifted, absent or other rose from 55.3% to 71.0% and 73.7%. Removals per 100 electors rose with each phase, from 8.3 to 12.9 and then 17.0.

State by state

Among states that removed ten lakh names or more, the share entered as shifted, absent or other runs from 49.7% in Andhra Pradesh to 91.1% in Delhi. Among them the share entered as dead is highest in West Bengal (41.5%), and the share entered as enrolled more than once in Andhra Pradesh (16.4%). One revision under one set of instructions produced very different mixes of reasons from state to state, and the Commission has not explained why.

Removals per 100 electors range from 2.5 in Lakshadweep to 32.8 in Delhi. Uttar Pradesh removed the most names in all, 2.89 crore.

Women among the removed

Only 11 states have published their removals by sex, or their roll by sex before the revision and on the draft. In 8 of the 11, women are a larger share of the names removed than of the roll, by up to 7.6 points in Bihar. Tamil Nadu, Karnataka and Chandigarh are within a point of even, and Delhi runs the other way, by 4.1 points. Most phase 3 states have published no split by sex, so this is not the national picture.

Across all states the removal rate goes most with the share of residents born in another state (0.51), and that comes from phase 3 (0.62). In phase 2 it is 0.04. In phase 3, income per head, Muslim share of households, sex ratio and share of residents born in another state all go with the removal rate, because the big states that draw migrants were revised in the same phase as the North-East, and they differ from it on all of these at once. With only 16 to 18 states in the phase, there is no telling these apart. The only characteristics that go with the removal rate the same way in both phases are ST share of households (-0.45 in phase 2, -0.26 in phase 3) and sex ratio (-0.36 in phase 2, -0.46 in phase 3). The Muslim share of households has no consistent relationship, 0.09 across all states, -0.27 in phase 2 and 0.46 in phase 3. Among the larger states, the share entered as enrolled more than once goes most with women's literacy (-0.70). Migration is from the 2011 census because no newer figure is published by state, and every other characteristic is from 2019 to 2026. Run on the 2011 census values instead, the reading changes only for urban share.

States on an international border removed fewer names than interior states. The typical border state removed 8.3 per 100 electors across 13 states, and the typical interior state 14.0 across 18. None of these relationships says who was removed inside a state, because a pattern across states tells us nothing about individuals.

After the final rolls

In the 21 states and union territories with a final roll, 7.57 crore names were left off the drafts, and the final rolls came out 1.07 crore larger than the drafts, which is 14.1% of the fall. West Bengal kept 60.1 lakh names off its final roll for adjudication. As reported, 32.7 lakh were later found eligible and 27.2 lakh not, and appeals continue. Without them the final rolls were 1.67 crore larger, 22.0% of the fall. The net fall is the roll before the revision less the final roll. It comes to 10.2 per 100 electors, against 11.9 left off at the draft.

The rise from draft to final counts new electors as well as names put back, and no state has said how many of those added had been removed at the draft, so it is the most that can have been put back. The final roll is smaller than the draft in 5 states (West Bengal, Goa, Arunachal Pradesh, Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu and Sikkim). How many names a state removed at the draft has no relationship with how much of that its final roll made up (rank correlation -0.15).

Against the winning margins

A seat is decided by the votes between its winner and its runner-up. Where more names left a seat's roll than that margin, the names removed were enough to decide the seat. Whether they did depends on how those people would have voted, and that can be estimated only where the lists say who they were.

In Bihar and Tamil Nadu, where the names removed are counted seat by seat, they were more than the winning margin in 385 of 477 assembly seats. West Bengal and Kerala publish no removals by seat, so their count is the fall in the roll, which also takes in ordinary deaths and moves, and it was more than the margin in 177 of 434. The two are kept apart because they count different things.

Bihar is the one state whose lists show who was removed. Estimating how they would have voted from their own booths in 2024, 4 to 6 seats would have changed hands, in both directions, and on every estimate the NDA keeps a majority, with 200 to 204 of 243 seats.

Set against the 2024 Lok Sabha results, the count in each seat was larger than the margin in 51 of 79 seats in Bihar and Tamil Nadu and 34 of 62 in West Bengal and Kerala. The revision came after the 2024 election, so this measures how large the removals are against the votes that decide a seat, and it does not say what happened in 2024.

No state has published its removals by Lok Sabha seat. Spread evenly over each state's seats, the removals would be larger than the 2024 margin in 373 of 516 seats in 31 states and union territories. Where seat counts exist the even spread can be tested. In Bihar it gives 32 seats against 32 counted, and in Tamil Nadu 27 against 19, because removals there were heaviest in the cities and most seats lost fewer names than the state average. It is a rough guide that can run high. In 2024, 115 of 542 contested seats were won by fewer than 50,000 votes.

The roll over forty years

The roll grew from 40.04 crore in 1984-85 to 97.98 crore in 2024. In every election to 1999 it held more names than there were people of voting age, up to 109.5 for every 100 in 1984-85. If the population estimate is right, a roll that large carried the names of people who had died or moved, or counted some people twice. From 2004 it has held fewer, 96 for every 100 in 2024. Some of that gap is people who are not citizens, whom the population counts and the roll cannot.

In the ten years from 2014 to 2024 the roll gained 14.57 crore. The 13.39 crore names left off the draft rolls are 92 in every 100 of that gain, though the two are not strictly alike, because the revision covered 31 states and union territories and the Lok Sabha figure is all India.

How the revision was run

The rules changed while the revision was under way. The site's page on how the revision was run lists 35 orders, letters and judgments in date order, 12 of them from the Supreme Court, each linked to its document, and draws every state's schedule as it was set and as it was moved. Across 32 states and union territories, dates were changed 29 times in the orders and notes that could be read.

What made it harder to stay on the roll, each point resting on an order or a judgment:

  1. A fresh form from every elector. Only electors whose enumeration forms came back were put on the draft roll. The enumeration form was a fresh form each elector on the roll had to fill in and return. The draft roll was the roll published from those forms, open to claims to add names and objections to names on it before the final roll. A name on the roll for years was left off the draft if its form did not come back in time, and its owner then had to ask to be put back.
  2. Parents' documents, by year of birth. In Bihar the younger the elector, the more documents were needed. Anyone born after 2 December 2004 needed documents for themselves and for both parents, and a parent's passport and visa if a parent was not Indian.
  3. Aadhaar only on the Court's order. Aadhaar was not on the first list of eleven documents. Claims could carry it from mid-August 2025, as the Commission agreed before the Supreme Court. It became the twelfth document on the Court's order of 8 September 2025, as proof of identity.
  4. The declaration changed twice. Form 6, the application to be added to the roll, carried a new declaration from June 2025, a different one from October 2025, and a shorter list of documents from May 2026. No Gazette notification could be found amending the forms of the Registration of Electors Rules, 1960 to match.
  5. Dates moved after they were set. All 12 Phase II states and union territories had their dates moved by a week, and 7 were moved again. In Phase III, the orders and notes read for this page show dates moved in 7 of 19. An elector working to one deadline could find it had changed.
  6. Anyone could object to anyone. Form 7 is the form for objecting to a name on the roll. A person who was not an elector of the constituency could object to a name on oath, a party agent could file ten forms a day after the draft, and Form 7 sets no limit on how many one person may file. In Bihar, objections to names on the roll outnumbered claims to be included by about six to one at the deadline.
  7. Notices for logical discrepancies. People flagged for logical discrepancies, where something in their link to the old roll did not match, were called to hearings. The Supreme Court had to order that a representative could file for them and that they get ten more days. Only from September 2026 would booth officers collect their documents at home.
  8. Sixty lakh held back in West Bengal. West Bengal's final roll left out 60,06,675 names marked under adjudication. They waited on judicial officers and then on nineteen tribunals, and a pending appeal gave no right to vote.
  9. Names added after the draft. All 18 states that published both counts added fewer names after the draft, new electors included, than they removed at it, and 3 added fewer than 10 for every 100 removed.

The page also lists 36 press reports of problems in the field, each attributed to its outlet and kept apart from the points above because no document confirms them.

What the Election Commission should do

13.39 crore names came off the draft rolls, about four in five of them on an entry that the elector had moved, could not be found, or was on the roll twice. The Election Commission should:

  1. Publish a state-by-state table of its own, in exact figures, with the reason for every removal at the draft and after it.
  2. Publish the deletion lists with the reason against each name for every state, as it did for Bihar, so that removals can be counted by booth, sex and age.
  3. Publish the basis for entries marked absent and enrolled more than once.
  4. Publish the outcome of every claim and objection, and say how many of those added at the final roll had been removed at the draft, so that restorations can be set against removals.
  5. Explain why the mix of reasons differs so widely between states under one set of instructions.

Method and sources

Each state's Chief Electoral Officer, the Election Commission's officer in charge of its rolls, published figures when the draft roll came out, and the All India figures are those officers' own. They are taken from the press notes where those could be read (Uttar Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal, Rajasthan, Lakshadweep, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Delhi, Andhra Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Meghalaya, Arunachal Pradesh, Manipur, Nagaland, Mizoram and Sikkim) and from press reports otherwise. Bihar's row is counted from the deletion lists. The reasons are grouped into the three every state reported. The state rows add up to 13.39 crore removed, against the about 13.3 crore reported nationally on 30 September 2026, and the difference is rounding. Each row's source is listed on the method page of the site and in the downloadable table.

No name, EPIC number or address is published.
