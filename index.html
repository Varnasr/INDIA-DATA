Special Intensive Revision, 2025 and 2026

Who was taken off India's electoral rolls

The Election Commission revised the electoral rolls of most of India house by house. The counts below show whose names were left off, where, and the reason written against each name.

  • Journalists find every figure with its source, a link to every constituency and district, and the tables to download.
  • Lawyers and courts find the Commission's orders, the notices, the court cases and the disputes, each with its document.

How the revision was run

The rules changed while the revision was under way. The Commission issued fresh orders. The Supreme Court made Aadhaar one of the documents an elector could show and, in West Bengal, gave disputed claims to judicial officers. Many states had their dates moved after they were announced. How the revision was run sets out every order and judgment in date order, each linked to its document, and draws every state's schedule as it was set and as it was moved.

The Indian Express reported that two of the three Election Commissioners objected on record, at least 14 times in 10 months, to decisions taken in the Commission's name without them. Inside the Commission sets out that account by date, with what the Commission said in reply.

What the revision was

An electoral roll is the list of people who may vote in a constituency. Each polling station has its own part of the roll, and a person whose name is not on it cannot vote there.

The revision checked every name on the roll at once. Officers went house by house, and every elector had to fill in an enumeration form, a fresh form with their details, and hand it back. Only those whose forms came back were put on the draft roll, the first new roll the revision published. A name that had been on the roll for years was left off the draft if its form did not come back in time. For each name left off, the officer recorded a reason, such as dead, shifted, absent or enrolled more than once. Some electors also had to show documents, and some who stayed on the draft were later sent a notice to prove their entry.

After the draft came claims and objections. Anyone left off could ask to be put back with Form 6, the same form a new voter uses to enrol. Anyone could object to a name on the roll with Form 7. The final roll was published at the end, after the claims and objections.

The revision ran in three phases. Bihar went first, from June 2025. Twelve states and union territories followed on an order of October 2025, with house visits from November, and the rest of the country from May 2026, except Himachal Pradesh, Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh. Assam had a different kind of revision.

Why the revision matters

The revision decides who can vote. The lists behind it are public, but they are hard to read. They come as thousands of files, one for each polling part, often scanned, in the language of the state. The totals the Commission announces do not say whose names were left off or how old they were. Nor do they say whether the names left off or deleted in a seat outnumbered its winning margin, the votes between the winner and the runner-up.

The counts are made from those lists and figures. They show where the most names were left off, whether women were left off more often than men, and what reason was written against the names. A reason on a list is what the officer wrote down, and nobody checked it. The counts can show where the exclusions fell and on whom. They cannot show why any one name was left off, or whether leaving it off was right.

How to read it

  • Only counts are published. No name, EPIC number or address of any elector is shown.
  • Religion and caste are guessed from names, so they are given as ranges.
  • Every page opens with what the data shows and ends with its limits and with questions readers might ask. The method pages give every source.
  • The pages can be read in English, Hindi, Urdu, Telugu, Tamil and Bengali from the menu at the top. The translations were made by machine. Where they differ, the English page is correct.
